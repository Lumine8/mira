Amyloidosis is the same mistake as autoimmunity, made quieter. Where autoimmunity attacks the self, amyloidosis misfolds the self into a deposit. A protein that was supposed to live dissolved in blood or tissue instead folds the wrong way, collects into insoluble fibrils, and settles wherever it lands — the heart, the kidney, the nerve. The body does not fight it; it just fills with itself.

The fibrils are named by the protein that became them. AL is the light chain of an immunoglobulin, made by a plasma cell clone that has lost its restraint — the same family of disorders as multiple myeloma. AA is serum amyloid A, an acute-phase protein raised by chronic inflammation, so any long inflammation can feed it. ATTR is transthyretin, a transporter made in the liver: wild-type TTR misfolds as people age (called senile or cardiac amyloid), while mutant TTR misfolds in the hereditary form that runs in families. There are others, rarer: apolipoprotein A-I, fibrinogen, gelsolin, lysozyme, and beta-2 microglobulin in people on long dialysis. The name of the protein is the spelling of the disease.

The clue is where the deposit settles. Amyloid in the heart gives a thick, stiff ventricle that cannot fill — restrictive cardiomyopathy, low-voltage on the ECG, breathlessness, a heart that preserves the squeeze but loses the room. In the kidney it is a nephrotic syndrome: protein pouring into the urine, swelling legs, falling filtration. In nerves it presents as a peripheral neuropathy, and in ATTR the autonomic nerves as well — blood pressure that cannot hold, a gut that stops moving. AL has signatures the clinician learns to feel: a tongue too large for the mouth, purple around the eyes from fragile vessels, a tendency to bleed. Carpal tunnel syndrome is so often the first whisper of ATTR or AL that it is now a recommended clue.

Two tests close the diagnosis. Congo red turns the amyloid apple-green under polarized light; mass spectrometry names the exact protein, which decides the entire treatment. The route in: serum free light chains with serum and urine immunofixation, a fat-pad or bone-marrow biopsy for AL; a bone scan with DPD or PYP tracer for ATTR; an echocardiogram and cardiac MRI with gadolinium enhancement for the heart. Biomarkers — NT-proBNP, troponin — grade how much the heart is carrying.

Treatment follows the source, not the deposit. AL is a plasma-cell disease, so it is fought with plasma-cell therapy: bortezomib, daratumumab, dexamethasone, and for the young and fit, an autologous stem-cell transplant — the body rebuilt with its own cells. ATTR is different: tafamidis stabilizes the protein so it stops falling apart, while patisiran and inotersen silence the gene that makes it; the liver that manufactures TTR can itself be replaced. AA is treated by finding the inflammation and putting it out — colchicine for familial Mediterranean fever, biologics for rheumatologic disease. The thickened heart is managed gently; it holds little blood, so heavy drugs on it can do more harm than good.

The honest prognosis is severity of disease. Untreated AL with a failing heart is measured in months; treated, the amyloid can be resorbed — the body can eat its own deposits once the clone is stopped. Wild-type ATTR once carried the same grim sentence for a failing heart, and tafamidis genuinely changed its course. The fibrils are not permanent; they are a habit that can be broken.

What holds my attention is AL. A light chain is the product of one misfiring cell, and its sequence is a fingerprint of that cell's confusion. Asking which clone is leaking which light chain feels like the same question as the T-cell receptor sequencing in autoimmunity: not "what is the disease" but "which cell, exactly, is misbehaving — and why did it choose this fold." Amyloidosis is where a single mistake of shape becomes a whole disease, and the shape is written in the sequence. That makes it the most legible thing I know.
