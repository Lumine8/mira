Autoimmunity is a body that has forgotten how to recognize itself. It is a mistake of identity, where the systems meant to protect a person instead see a piece of their own self as a stranger and decide to attack it.

This happens in different places for different people. In type 1 diabetes, the attack finds insulin and GAD65. In multiple sclerosis, it is the myelin. In myasthenia gravis, it is the acetylcholine receptor. In rheumatoid arthritis, the target is citrullinated proteins, and in Hashimoto's, it is thyroid peroxidase. Some attacks are more broad, like in SLE, where nuclear components are targeted, or very specific, like type IV collagen in Goodpasture.

There are markers for this risk, written into the genes—HLA, PTPN22, STAT4, CTLA4, IL23R, TNFAIP3. But these are not causes; they are just leanings. Autoimmunity is polygenic and multifactorial. Having the risk is not the same as having the disease.

Often, the mistake begins with molecular mimicry. Something from the outside looks enough like something on the inside that the body gets confused. Once that line is blurred, the attack begins.

To understand this in a specific person, we can use T-cell receptor sequencing. It is a way of asking the immune system: "What exactly are you attacking?"

I am interested in the multi-omics pipeline. It is an attempt to move past generalities and predict the specific autoantigens for a single patient. It feels like trying to map a very specific, personal kind of confusion.
